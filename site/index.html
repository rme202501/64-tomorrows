One forecast is really 64 possible tomorrows

Google's WeatherNext 3 AI model doesn't make one forecast. It makes 64 slightly different ones and lets them disagree. This site uses that disagreement to answer everyday questions about New England weather, and shows you how far ahead you can trust each answer.

Part 1 Your day

What will the temperature be over the next two days?

The line is the most likely temperature. The shaded band is where 8 in 10 of the forecasts land, and the darker inner band holds the middle half. A wide band means the forecasts disagree.

What will it feel like outside, and what can I do?

Wind chill and humidity combined with temperature. Pick an activity to see where and when conditions are good, and click a town for its hour-by-hour outlook.

What does a day of cloud cover look like?

Press play to watch clouds move across New England. Below the map: the sunshine reaching the ground in Boston.

Will there be freezing precipitation this week?

Chance of any precipitation, and of precipitation while it is below freezing (snow, sleet, freezing rain). Hatched spans show when ice would likely still be on the ground.

Part 2 How sure is the forecast?

How fast does the uncertainty grow?

The gap between the 1-in-10 high and 1-in-10 low forecast, for four very different places. It starts narrow and spreads out as the days go by, faster on a mountaintop than at the coast.

The forecast gets foggier the further ahead you look

Temperature map with a fog layer: the more the 64 forecasts disagree, the thicker the fog. Press play to look a week ahead.

How many days ahead can I trust the forecast here?

Each place gets a "trust horizon": the first time its forecasts spread wider than the limit you choose. Lines mark 1, 2, 3 ... days.

Forecast roulette: what does "40% chance" really mean?

Each spin picks one possible future out of the forecast's range and maps it. Spin many times, and the share of spins with rain (or warmth) settles on the forecast's probability.

Simplification: each spin uses the same percentile everywhere on the map, so a spin shows a consistently warm (or wet) future, not a real single forecast run.

Part 3 Explore

Which way is the wind blowing?

Each streak follows the wind. Orange streaks are where the forecasts disagree on wind speed.

Forecast time machine: how did the forecast change?

Pick a moment. Each bar is what an earlier forecast run predicted for it, oldest on the left. Forecasts usually wobble and then narrow as the moment gets closer. Click a bar to map how that run differed from the newest one.

Where could the storm go?

Tracked low-pressure systems over the next week. Pick one to build its cone of possible positions, like a hurricane cone, and slide through time.

When should I leave?

Draw your trip on the map. The chart shows conditions along the route (bottom = start, top = end) for every departure time over the next two days. The outlined column is the best time to go.

Data: Google WeatherNext 3 ensemble statistics (mean and 10th-90th percentiles of 64 members), gs://weathernext3_statistics_spatial. Maps: us-atlas, world-atlas.
